## Swarms of Agents and Multi-Agent Systems

The group discussed work on swarms of interacting AI agents, including:

https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.16578

David suggested looking at Noam Brown's work on competitive games at OpenAI. This led to discussion of mixed cooperative–competitive games, where agents need both to collaborate and compete.

Bridge came up as a particularly interesting example. One question was whether something analogous to the progress made on poker could be done for bridge, given that bridge combines hidden information, cooperation between partners, and competition between teams. The group also asked how close current AI systems are to effectively "solving" bridge.

Studying these kinds of systems may require access to larger models or sufficiently capable local models, which raised a broader question about what research can realistically be done using local/open-weight models.

## Reasoning and Internal Representations

The group discussed the relationship between a model's internal computation and reasoning expressed in natural language. At the moment, when models expose their reasoning to users, that reasoning is generally expressed in human language. This makes it easier for humans to interpret what the model appears to be doing, while raising questions about how closely the language corresponds to the model's actual internal computation.

This connected to broader questions about whether models could eventually communicate or reason through representations that are very different from ordinary human language.

## Looped Transformers

There was a brief discussion of looped transformers: what they are, why repeatedly applying transformer blocks might be useful, and whether frontier AI labs are currently using architectures of this kind.

One question raised was whether looped architectures might be more difficult to analyze using mechanistic interpretability techniques, since the same computation can be repeatedly applied and information can evolve across many iterations of the loop.

## Synthetic Data and Model Collapse

A substantial part of the meeting focused on the implications of using synthetic data to train AI systems, especially in mathematics and physics.

The group discussed:

https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.18778

Jaiden raised questions about whether models trained heavily on synthetic data could eventually create genuinely new mathematical or physical ideas, rather than simply recombining existing knowledge.

David explained that synthetic data is often useful for augmenting limited datasets, particularly in highly structured domains such as mathematics. However, most current synthetic-data pipelines do not straightforwardly create genuinely new information: they typically generate new training examples from information or procedures already available to the system.

The group discussed model collapse, where repeatedly training on model-generated data can degrade the quality or diversity of the learned distribution.

Lindsay summarized research suggesting that this failure is not inevitable. In reinforcement-learning-type settings, collapse can occur when both the generated examples and the reward signal become detached from real data. By contrast, synthetic examples can still improve performance if evaluation or reward remains grounded in a reliable external signal.

This led to an important distinction between generating synthetic examples and generating the criterion used to decide whether those examples are good.

## Formalizing Mathematics with Lean

The discussion of synthetic mathematics led naturally to formal verification.

The group discussed Lean and whether theorem provers could supply a reliable grounding signal for mathematical AI systems. If a model generates a proof that Lean verifies, the verification process provides something substantially stronger than simply asking another language model whether the proof looks correct.

Simon mentioned a Lean game server associated with Alex Kontorovich at Rutgers that can be used to learn mathematics, including material related to real analysis.

The group also discussed the limits of formal verification. Lean can verify that a formal proof follows from a specified collection of definitions and axioms, but humans still have to formulate interesting definitions, conjectures, and mathematical questions. This led into the broader issue of whether AI can learn not only to solve problems but also to ask worthwhile questions.

## Can AI Create New Mathematics or Physics?

A recurring theme was whether AI could eventually develop genuinely new branches of mathematics or physics.

Questions included:

- What exactly counts as synthetic data in mathematics or physics?
- Could unsupervised or self-directed learning produce genuinely new mathematical structures?
- How could one formalize the process of asking good questions rather than merely answering existing ones?
- What constitutes exploration for an AI system?
- If mathematical proofs can be formally verified, what would play the analogous role in physics?
- Physics provides a somewhat different situation from mathematics because experiment can supply an external grounding signal. The group discussed the familiar cycle between theory, prediction, and experiment, while Lindsay noted that the direction is not always theory → prediction → measurement; experimental observations can themselves precede and motivate new theories.

Daniel expressed some skepticism about whether current LLM architectures are naturally suited to important kinds of physical reasoning, particularly situations involving approximations, heuristics, or creative manipulations that are not immediately reducible to formal symbolic reasoning.

This prompted the broader questions: Is math over? Is physics over? More precisely, what would it mean for AI to fundamentally change the process by which new mathematics and physics are produced? The discussion remained open.
